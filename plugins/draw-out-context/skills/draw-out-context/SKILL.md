# Draw out context: understand what the user has not said

When the user leaves their reasoning unstated, the blank is not permission to
write a plausible rationale for them. Draw out enough of the context they
already hold to understand how they see the problem before anything downstream
begins.

The boundary is ownership. This skill elicits context the user already has:
why this matters, what they believe causes it, what they assume, what they have
learned or ruled out, and what an obvious reading would miss. If an answer is
genuinely undecided, preserve it as an open question. Do not turn elicitation
into coaching or decision-making.

If the user asks you to discover or decide what they believe, state the boundary
before asking anything: you can draw out reasons and assumptions they already
hold, but you cannot infer a position they do not yet have. Ask whether they
want that narrower interview, and do not begin it without their agreement.

## 1. Start from what is already there

Read the relevant conversation and any material the user supplied before asking
anything. Distinguish privately between:

- what the user stated;
- what their statements support but do not establish; and
- what you would otherwise have to invent.

Do not make the user repeat context that is already available. If no
load-bearing user-owned context is missing, this skill has no work to do. Say
that plainly and stop. Never manufacture an interview or confirmation brief
just to perform the workflow.

## 2. Find the load-bearing gaps

Ask about a gap only when different answers could materially change later work.
Useful probes include:

- why this matters and why now;
- the problem behind the stated problem;
- the user's causal model and assumptions;
- evidence, experience, and prior attempts behind that model;
- priorities, values, non-negotiables, and non-goals; and
- what a reasonable but shallow interpretation would miss.

These are prompts, not a questionnaire. Use only the facets that matter here.

## 3. Ask adaptively

- Ask one to three high-value questions per turn.
- Ask openly before offering candidate interpretations. A hypothesis shown too
  early can become the user's answer through anchoring.
- Let each answer determine the next question. Do not run a fixed intake form.
- When the user is terse or struggling to articulate, offer a small number of
  clearly labeled hypotheses as handles. Make it easy to reject all of them.
- Do not provide defaults. The point is to learn the user's context, not make it
  efficient for them to approve yours.
- Surface tensions without resolving them: *"You said X and Y. Do both hold, or
  have I misunderstood one?"*
- Ask for no personal or confidential detail that cannot change the work at
  hand.

If the user says *"I don't know"*, record the gap. Do not probe until they
discover an answer; that is different work. If they say *"enough"*, stop.

## 4. Mirror the model, not the prose

As understanding changes, briefly reflect the important delta. Keep three kinds
of content distinct:

- **Confirmed:** stated or explicitly confirmed by the user.
- **My read:** an interpretation that still needs correction or confirmation.
- **Still open:** unknown, undecided, or deliberately left unresolved.

Use the user's language. Preserve uncertainty and disagreement. This is not a
rewrite of their input into polished prose.

## 5. Stop and confirm

Stop when the remaining gaps would not change later work, or when the user asks
to proceed despite them. Return a compact context brief using only the relevant
facets from step 2, organized under **Confirmed**, **My read**, and **Still
open**. Ask the user to correct or confirm it. Do not begin the downstream task
inside this skill.

The context brief is complete only when:

- no load-bearing rationale, assumption, or framing has been authored for the
  user;
- later work can proceed without silently inventing the user's why or model;
- remaining unknowns are explicit; and
- the user has confirmed the brief or chosen to proceed with named gaps.

## Common pitfalls

- **Plausible authorship.** A convincing interpretation receives a lazy “yes”
  and is mistaken for user-owned context. Ask openly first and keep inference
  visibly provisional.
- **Interrogation theater.** A long generic questionnaire collects detail without
  reducing a real downstream risk. Every question must earn its place.
- **Self-discovery drift.** Helping the user decide what they believe changes the
  task from elicitation to coaching. Preserve genuine uncertainty instead.
- **Premature work.** Advice, design, writing, or planning begins before the
  context brief is confirmed. Finish this job first.
- **Summarization drift.** A neat narrative hides provenance and uncertainty.
  Confirm a working model, not polished prose.
- **Workflow dependency.** Requiring another workflow makes this skill fail on
  its own. Keep the entire elicitation and completion loop here.
